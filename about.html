About

Two months coaching football in Lusaka is what actually changed how I see travel. Victoria Falls was staggering. The safaris were the kind of thing you don't quite believe even while you're in them. But the part that stayed with me longest was smaller than that — the five-a-side games on dirt pitches after school, the walk to the market, conversations with people who had no reason to explain their day-to-day life to a stranger from England, but did anyway. That's where I actually started paying attention.

I'm not claiming any of that as hardship, and I'm well aware of how fortunate I am to have been able to go at all. What it gave me instead was a genuine appetite — for the places that don't make it onto a list of things to see, and for the version of a country that exists whether or not anyone's photographing it. I think most places get flattened into a handful of images before you ever arrive: one landmark, one view, one feeling. They deserve more curiosity than that, and so do the people living there.

So this is what I'm trying to do with a camera: put places on the map — the famous view, because it earned its fame, but also what's three streets away from it. The market, the classroom, the walk home from training. Not instead of the landmarks. Alongside them. A place is never just its most photographed moment, and I'd rather show you both than pretend the postcard is the whole story.

I only picked up a camera properly for the first time on a trip through Sofia to Santorini — everything on this site since has come out of that.
